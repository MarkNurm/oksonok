Manufacture

Raw Materials

Large quantities of raw materials are needed to manufacture digital devices — including arsenic, copper, and palladium. Mining scars the landscape and toxic chemicals contaminate water supplies. Mining machinery releases large amounts of CO₂.

Mining Impacts

  • Mining permanently scars and destroys landscapes
  • Toxic runoff contaminates nearby water supplies
  • Heavy mining machinery releases large volumes of CO₂
  • Habitat destruction damages local ecosystems
  • Many materials are classified as conflict minerals

A single smartphone requires around 70+ different elements from the periodic table. Many are conflict minerals, mined in dangerous conditions with little environmental regulation.

Energy Consumption

Vast amounts of energy are consumed in the production and functioning of digital equipment, as well as in data centres that power cloud services and the internet.

Device Manufacturing

Producing a single laptop can consume as much energy as years of normal operation. Fabricating semiconductors and circuit boards involves highly energy-intensive chemical processes.

Data Centres

Global data centres consume around 200–250 TWh of electricity per year — roughly 1% of global electricity use. They require enormous cooling systems running 24/7 just to prevent overheating.

Network Infrastructure

Routers, switches, base stations and undersea cables require continuous power. The internet's global network infrastructure consumes hundreds of terawatt-hours of electricity annually.

Device Use

Billions of devices running simultaneously — computers, phones, TVs, smart appliances — collectively add up to a significant portion of global electricity demand every year.

Disposal & E-waste

Toxic Landfill

When devices are dumped in landfill, toxic materials like lead, mercury, and cadmium leach into soil and groundwater, causing long-term environmental and public health damage to surrounding communities.

Exported E-waste

Wealthy nations often export e-waste to developing countries in West Africa and South/East Asia, where it is burned or broken down by hand with little worker protection, releasing toxic fumes.

Section 02 · Responsibility & Positive Impact

Environmental Issues 2

How we can take responsibility for device lifecycles, reduce energy use, and the ways digital technology actually helps the environment.

Short Replacement Cycle

The Problem

People replace smartphones on average every 2–3 years and laptops every 4–5 years — often because of manufacturer pressure rather than device failure. This drives massive and growing e-waste volumes globally.

Planned Obsolescence

  • Manufacturers slow older devices via software updates
  • Batteries become non-replaceable, reducing lifespan
  • Spare parts become unavailable after a few years
  • "Fashion" cycles incentivise unnecessary upgrades
Responsible Ownership

Repair First

Before replacing, attempt repair. Many faults (cracked screens, dead batteries) are fixable. The Right to Repair movement pushes for manufacturers to support this legally.

Sell or Donate

A working device can be resold or donated. Charities, schools, and developing-world programmes give old hardware a second life rather than sending it to landfill.

Proper Recycling

Use certified WEEE (Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment) recycling drop-offs. Many retailers offer trade-in and take-back schemes for responsible disposal.

Ways to Reduce Energy Consumption

At Home / Work

  • Enable sleep/hibernate modes on computers
  • Use power-efficient settings and dark mode on OLED screens
  • Turn off devices fully rather than leaving on standby
  • Choose Energy Star–rated equipment when buying
  • Unplug chargers when not in use — they draw phantom power

Organisationally

  • Virtualisation — run multiple virtual machines on one server
  • Move to cloud services with efficient shared infrastructure
  • Power data centres with renewable energy sources
  • Liquid cooling systems more efficient than air cooling
  • Go paperless — digital documents save paper and energy
Positive Impact of Digital Technology

Reducing Travel

Video conferencing, remote working, and online services reduce the need for physical travel — significantly cutting aviation and road transport carbon emissions.

Smart Agriculture

Sensors and data analytics optimise water and fertiliser use in farming, reducing waste and environmental impact while simultaneously improving crop yields.

Renewable Energy Management

Digital systems manage smart grids, balancing supply from wind and solar. AI helps predict demand and optimise energy distribution, enabling higher renewable usage.

Paperless Systems

Digital documents, e-tickets, and online billing have drastically reduced paper consumption — saving trees and the energy consumed in traditional print and postal processes.

Section 03 · Privacy & Digital Footprint

Personal Data

The data trail you leave online, who benefits from it, who owns it, and how you and organisations can protect it.

Digital Footprint

Your digital footprint is the trail of data you leave behind whenever you use the internet or digital services. It exists in two forms: active (data you deliberately share) and passive (data collected without your direct knowledge).

Active Footprint

  • Social media posts, comments, and likes
  • Online purchases and payment details
  • Emails and messages you send
  • Forum posts and reviews you leave
  • Profile information submitted to websites

Passive Footprint

  • Websites you browse (tracked via cookies)
  • Location data collected from your phone
  • Search history logged by search engines
  • Device and browser fingerprinting data
  • IP address logged by websites you visit

Owning the Data

The User's View

Many argue you own your own data. It describes your life, behaviour, and identity — a digital reflection of you that should be under your personal control.

The Company's View

Companies claim ownership because they built the platform and infrastructure. Terms & conditions grant them wide usage rights that users agree to, often without reading them.

The Legal View

Under GDPR and the DPA 2018, individuals have rights over their data — including the right to access, correct, and delete it. The ICO enforces these rights in the UK.

Right to be forgotten problem: Once data is shared or collected, it is very difficult to remove entirely. Deleted accounts may still leave data in backups, third-party copies, or cached pages — this is the core challenge of the "right to be forgotten."

Protecting Personal Data

What You Can Do

  • Use strong, unique passwords and a password manager
  • Enable two-factor authentication (2FA)
  • Review app permissions — disable unnecessary access
  • Use a VPN to mask your IP address
  • Clear cookies and browsing history regularly
  • Read (or skim) privacy policies before signing up

What Organisations Must Do

  • Store only data that is necessary (data minimisation)
  • Encrypt data in transit and at rest
  • Obtain clear, informed consent before collecting data
  • Notify users and regulators in the event of a breach
  • Appoint a Data Protection Officer (DPO) if required

Data Protection Act 2018 (DPA)
UK implementation of GDPR
2018

The DPA 2018 is the UK's implementation of the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). It governs how personal data must be collected, stored, and used by organisations.

The eight principles of data protection (GDPR-aligned):

1 Processed lawfully, fairly, and transparently
2 Collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes
3 Adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary
4 Accurate and kept up to date
5 Not kept longer than necessary
6 Processed securely with appropriate safeguards
7 Not transferred outside the UK without adequate protection
8 Accountability — organisations must demonstrate compliance

Individual rights under DPA/GDPR: Right to access data held about you · Right to rectification (correction) · Right to erasure ("right to be forgotten") · Right to restrict or object to processing · Right to data portability

Penalties for non-compliance can reach £17.5 million or 4% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher. The ICO (Information Commissioner's Office) enforces the Act in the UK.

Computer Misuse Act 1990 (CMA)
Updated 2015 for modern cybercrime
1990

The CMA criminalises unauthorised access to computer systems and data. It was introduced following high-profile hacking cases in the 1980s and has been updated to address modern cybercrime.

Section 1 — Unauthorised Access

Accessing a computer system without permission (e.g. guessing someone's password to read their emails). Up to 2 years imprisonment.

Section 2 — Access to Commit

Unauthorised access with intent to commit a further crime such as fraud or blackmail. Up to 5 years imprisonment.

Section 3 — Unauthorised Modification

Modifying or damaging computer material without permission — includes deploying malware, ransomware, or deleting data. Up to 10 years imprisonment.

Section 3ZA — Serious Damage (added 2015)

Attacks on critical national infrastructure — power grids, hospitals, water systems. Can result in a life sentence.

Cookies & PECR
Privacy & Electronic Communications Regulations
2003 / 2011

The Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations (PECR) sit alongside the DPA and govern cookies and electronic marketing.

What Are Cookies?

Small text files stored on your device by websites you visit. They remember your preferences, login state, shopping basket contents, and track your behaviour across the web.

Types of Cookies

  • Session cookies — deleted when the browser closes
  • Persistent cookies — remain for a set duration
  • First-party cookies — set by the site you visit
  • Third-party cookies — from advertisers and trackers

Under PECR, websites must obtain consent before setting non-essential cookies. Users must be told what cookies are used, why, and how long they last. Strictly necessary cookies (e.g. keeping you logged in) do not require consent.
